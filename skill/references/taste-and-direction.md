# Dipan's taste and direction

## Core profile

Dipan likes modern SaaS and product design that feels intentional rather than decorated. The preferred balance is minimal but informative: generous negative space and type-led hierarchy, followed by enough real product proof, workflow and developer value to make the page credible.

Minimal does not mean empty, monochrome or identical. It means every visible element earns its place.

## Positive signals

- One focal idea per section.
- Large, controlled type with good line breaks.
- Restrained color and one clear accent.
- Quiet dark surfaces or light editorial fields.
- Product screenshots cropped to the claim.
- A mix of overview, focused landscape and occasional portrait proof.
- Inset imagery with visible margin, subtle border and an inner radius.
- Purposeful local bento for comparison.
- Dense proof followed by sparse type-led pauses.
- Small, meaningful motion that shows status, hierarchy or continuity.
- Distinct page identities connected by shared quality rather than shared decoration.

## Negative signals

- The same rounded card repeated in every section.
- Images inserted to fill space rather than prove a claim.
- Tiny unreadable full-product screenshots.
- Coded mock interfaces shown next to real product proof.
- Repeated screenshots or duplicate image bytes.
- Heavy gradient, glow, pill, border or badge accumulation.
- Motion on every card, constant floating or scroll hijacking.
- Desktop compressed onto mobile without reordering or re-cropping.
- Generic startup copy, invented metrics or false logos.
- A design system that erases the product's character.

## Trained taste process

Taste is judgment developed by exposure, analysis and practice, not a list of fashionable properties.

1. Collect strong references from products relevant to the current problem.
2. Use the product. Do not judge only a screenshot.
3. Name the exact reason something works: hierarchy, spacing, crop, sequence, feedback, type contrast or restraint.
4. Compare several solutions to the same problem.
5. Rebuild the principle using the current product's real content.
6. Remove one layer of decoration and check whether clarity improves.
7. Render and critique the result at actual use sizes.

Keep a decision log. "Feels modern" is not a reason. "The wide proof lets the user compare four statuses without opening a carousel" is a reason.

## Quality hierarchy

1. **Clarity:** can the user understand the page or state?
2. **Coherence:** do type, spacing, color, imagery and interaction agree?
3. **Credibility:** is there honest, legible evidence for the claim?
4. **Delight:** do craft details make the interaction feel better without slowing it?

Never use delight to compensate for weak clarity or credibility.
